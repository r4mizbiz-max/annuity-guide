Saving money and creating income are different jobs

During your working years, the job was easy to describe: put money away and let it grow. Retirement changes the job.

Now the same savings may need to pay your bills for decades. Nobody can tell you in advance how long you'll live, what markets will do, or what things will cost.

That raises new questions. How much can I safely take out each month? What if the market falls just as I start withdrawing? What if I live much longer than I planned?

An annuity is one possible tool for part of this problem. It isn't right for everyone, and it doesn't need to hold all of your money. This guide explains how annuities work so you can decide whether one deserves a closer look.

What an annuity is

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. You pay the insurer, either all at once or over time. In return, the insurer makes the specific promises written in that contract.

Those promises can include crediting interest, paying income for a set number of years, or paying income for life. Some annuities start paying soon after you buy them. Others, called deferred annuities, hold your money first and pay out later, if you choose.

The word to remember is contract. Two annuities can work very differently. One may offer lifetime income. Another may offer it only if you convert the contract to payments or add an optional feature. Judge the contract in front of you, not what you've heard about annuities in general.

Three main types of annuity

Fixed annuity

The insurer credits interest at a rate stated in the contract for a set period. When that period ends, the rate may change, subject to any minimum in the contract. Market swings don't directly reduce your value, but early withdrawals can cost you.

Fixed indexed annuity

Interest is calculated by a formula tied to a market index. Your money is not invested in the index. You don't own the stocks in it, and you generally don't receive their dividends.

Three terms shape what you're credited. A cap limits the most interest you can earn in a period. A participation rate sets how much of the index's gain counts. A spread is subtracted from the gain. Insurers can often change these at renewal, within contract limits. In some periods, you may be credited nothing.

Variable annuity

Your money goes into investment options called subaccounts, which are similar to mutual funds. Your value rises and falls with them, and you can lose money, including your principal. Variable annuities are securities. They're sold with a prospectus, which you should read before deciding. Optional guarantees may be available at added cost.

Question 1What job would this money do?

Before you compare products, decide what this specific money is for. Most goals fit one of three jobs.

  • Income. Turning savings into regular payments. Ask how much, starting when, for how long, and what happens to the payments when you die.
  • Accumulation. Growing money over a number of years. Ask how growth is determined and how long the money must stay put.
  • Legacy. Leaving money to someone. Ask how the death benefit works, whether taking income reduces it, and how your beneficiaries would be taxed.

One contract rarely does all three equally well. A choice that increases income may reduce what passes to your family. Some life-only payouts stop when you die, even if little has been paid. Name the job first, then judge whether the contract does it.

Question 2How much access to the money will I need?

Annuities are generally built for long-term money. Getting out early can be expensive, so plan for access before you sign.

Surrender periods

Many deferred annuities charge a surrender fee if you take out more than the allowed amount during the first several years. The length and size of the charge depend on the contract, and the charge often shrinks over time. Ask to see the schedule, year by year.

Withdrawal limits

Many contracts let you withdraw a limited amount each year without a surrender charge. Taking more can trigger the charge. Withdrawals can also reduce an income benefit or death benefit, and they may have tax consequences. Some contracts waive charges for events such as a nursing home stay or terminal illness, but only if the contract says so.

If you convert a contract into a stream of payments, you may give up access to a lump sum altogether.

Emergency liquidity

Before you put money into any annuity, set aside separate money you can reach without penalty for repairs, medical bills and family needs. Then try this test: if I needed a large sum next year, where would it come from? If the only answer is the annuity, stop and rethink.

Many states also require a short free-look period after delivery, when you can cancel. Ask how long yours lasts and how any refund is calculated.

Question 3What costs and trade-offs apply?

Costs depend on the contract, so ask for them in writing. Some are easy to see. Others are built in.

  • Contract fees. Some contracts charge annual administrative fees. Variable annuities often add insurance charges and investment expenses. A contract with few visible fees may carry its cost in a lower rate or cap.
  • Optional riders. Income and death-benefit riders usually cost extra each year. Ask what the charge is calculated on, and whether you pay it even if you never use the benefit.
  • Exit costs. These include surrender charges and, in some contracts, a market value adjustment that can change what you receive if you leave early.
  • Agent compensation. Agents are commonly paid a commission by the insurer. You may not get a separate bill, but it's part of how the product is priced. Commission isn't wrong. It's simply fair to ask how you're paid on this contract, and whether that would differ on another option.

Finally, ask what you'd give up. Protection, income features and access all come at a price, often paid through limited growth or limited access. Ask: compared with keeping this money where it is, what do I gain and what do I lose?

Question 4What exactly is guaranteed, under what conditions, and by whom?

When you hear the word "guaranteed," ask three follow-up questions.

  • What is guaranteed? A minimum interest rate, protection from index losses and a lifetime payment are separate promises. One doesn't imply the others.
  • Under what conditions? A guarantee may depend on buying a rider, waiting a certain time, reaching a certain age, or staying within withdrawal limits. Taking too much can reduce or end an income benefit.
  • By whom? Guarantees depend on the claims-paying ability of the issuing insurance company. Annuities are not bank deposits and are not FDIC-insured. Ask how independent rating agencies rate the insurer.

Two numbers that aren't the same

A contract with an income rider may show two values. They do different things, so keep them apart.

Income benefit baseA calculation number used to work out future income under a rider. You generally can't withdraw it as cash.
Cash available to withdrawWhat you'd actually receive if you left, after any surrender charges and adjustments.

A benefit base can grow at a stated rate while your cash value does something different. If you see a large number on an illustration, ask: can I take this out as cash today? Be just as careful with bonuses, which may apply only to the benefit base or come with longer restrictions.

Not sure what a guarantee in an illustration really covers? Ask how it works, what it depends on and who stands behind it.

Question 5How do taxes work?

Tax treatment depends on the money you use and how you take it out. This is general information, not tax advice.

  • Qualified contracts are funded with retirement money, such as a traditional IRA or 401(k). If the money went in pre-tax, withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income. Required minimum distribution rules may apply. Roth accounts follow different rules.
  • Nonqualified contracts are bought with after-tax money. What you paid in is your after-tax basis, which isn't taxed again when you get it back. Earnings are generally taxed as ordinary income when you withdraw them.

Timing matters too. With withdrawals from most nonqualified contracts, earnings generally come out first. If you convert to regular annuity payments, each payment usually includes part earnings and part return of basis.

Early distributions. Taking taxable money before age 59½ can trigger a 10% additional federal tax on top of regular income tax. There are exceptions, such as death, disability and certain series of substantially equal payments. Which ones apply depends on the type of contract or account. State taxes may also apply.

Inside an IRA or 401(k). These accounts are already tax-deferred, so an annuity inside one adds no extra tax deferral. Any reason to use one there has to come from something else, such as an income feature.

Beneficiaries may also owe income tax on earnings they inherit. Take your individual questions to a tax professional before you act.

When an annuity may not fit

Walking away can be the right decision. An annuity may be a poor match if:

  • Your time horizon is short. You may need this money within the next few years or before a surrender period ends.
  • Your emergency savings are thin. Build that cushion first.
  • You need unrestricted access. A contract that limits withdrawals conflicts with that need.
  • Dependable income already covers your essentials, and you aren't trying to solve an income problem.
  • You can't explain it. If you can't describe in your own words how it works and how you'd get out, you aren't ready.
  • You feel rushed. A good decision can survive a night's sleep and a second opinion.

Not sure which side of this list you're on? A consultation can end with "this isn't a fit," and that's a useful answer.

If you already own an annuity

Understand what you have before you change anything. Gather your latest statement and your contract, including any rider pages. Then review:

  • Any remaining surrender charges and when they end
  • What you pay for riders each year
  • The benefits you hold, including any death benefit
  • Your income options and when they begin
  • Your cash value, as distinct from any benefit base

Be careful with offers to replace a contract. A replacement can restart surrender restrictions, add new costs and lose valuable benefits, including older terms that may no longer be available.

A 1035 exchange can move value from one annuity to another without immediate tax when the rules are met. That only addresses taxes. It doesn't make a new contract better. Neither a replacement nor an exchange should be automatic. Ask for a written side-by-side comparison of what you'd keep, lose and pay.

Already own an annuity? Ask what your current contract does before you change anything. Keeping it may be the right answer.
